## Context

Boston Health Care for the Homeless Program (BHCHP) delivers care to people experiencing homelessness across Boston. Like many nonprofits, it leans heavily on student learners and volunteers — but recruiting them ran on a **decentralized, manual, time-intensive process**: applications arrived through scattered channels, discipline administrators tracked them by hand, and required agreements and waivers were chased down one email at a time.

Every hour staff spent shepherding paperwork was an hour not spent on the community they serve.

## The problem

There was no single place to apply, track a status, or manage the pipeline. Applications weren't reliably routed to the right discipline (Nursing, Medicine, and others), status was opaque to applicants, and compliance — proof of affiliation, immunization records, background checks — lived in people's heads and inboxes.

**How might we** give applicants a clear, self-serve path and give administrators a routed, low-overhead way to review and onboard them?

## My role

I led design as part of a Code4Community team (a PM, a tech lead, engineers, and four BHCHP stakeholders). I owned the research synthesis, information architecture, end-to-end flows, and the hi-fi UI for both sides of the product — the public applicant experience and the internal admin dashboard — and partnered closely with the tech lead to keep the design buildable against a real backend.

<blockquote>
The design had to serve two very different users at once: a first-time student applicant who wants clarity, and an overloaded administrator who wants to move fast.
</blockquote>

## Process

**Key insight — the pipeline, not the form, was the bottleneck.** It was tempting to treat this as "build a nicer application form." Discovery with staff reframed it: the real cost was in *routing, review, and compliance follow-up*. That pushed the design toward a status-driven system with a clear state machine — Submitted → In Review → Accepted/Rejected → Forms Sent → Completed → Final Approval — rather than a prettier intake.

**Directions considered.** We weighed (a) a lightweight form + shared spreadsheet, (b) an off-the-shelf form tool with manual handoffs, and (c) a purpose-built portal with role-based access and automated form generation. We diverged toward (c) because discipline-based routing and secure document handling — non-negotiable for a healthcare org — couldn't be met credibly by the lighter options.

**The decision that mattered most — role-based visibility.** Discipline administrators must see *only* their discipline's applicants; higher-level admins oversee everything. Designing around that constraint early shaped the whole IA, the dashboard filters, and how discipline changes reset a review.

## The solution

A single portal with three surfaces:

<Figure placeholder="Applicant flow — application → account → status timeline (drop in from Figma)" wide />

- **Public application form** — routes each submission to the right discipline, validates inline, prevents duplicate accounts, and confirms by email within minutes.
- **Applicant portal** — a status timeline, a documents-and-forms tab with live statuses, secure uploads, and clear "action required" prompts.
- **Admin dashboard** — role-scoped list views with filters by discipline, status, and date; an application detail view; accept/reject with reason capture; and an auto-populated required-forms packet on acceptance.

### Key decisions and why

- **A visible status timeline over a plain "pending" state.** Applicants abandon when they can't tell what's happening; making every state legible is the single biggest lever on the ≥80% completion target.
- **Auto-populated form packets driven by a rules engine.** Rather than admins hand-assembling agreements per discipline and affiliation, acceptance pre-fills the correct packet — with the option to add or remove before sending. It cuts the most error-prone manual step.
- **Compliance as a first-class checklist, not an afterthought.** "Final Approval" stays disabled until every discipline-specific item is met — so the system enforces what used to rely on memory.

### Craft & edge cases

Button hover/active/disabled states are clearly distinguished; errors surface inline with descriptive, fix-oriented text; drafts auto-save with toast confirmation. Accessibility was scoped in, not bolted on — sufficient contrast, full keyboard navigation, and ARIA labeling throughout.

## Outcome

The team defined success up front (a credibility point worth stating honestly):

- **≥80%** application completion rate
- **&lt;5%** error rate in submission/processing
- **&lt;2s** average page load; **&lt;30s** for document-status sync

Backend MVP is targeted for end of Fall 2025 and the frontend for end of Spring 2026, with iterative usability testing through spring–summer and a **soft launch for affiliated learners in summer 2026**.

**An honest gap:** the MVP intentionally accepts only institution-affiliated learners and leaves shift scheduling out of scope. Open questions I'd still want to resolve with the client — how school affiliation is validated, and the document-retention policy for signed PDFs — are exactly the kind of details that decide whether the system is trusted in practice.

## Reflection

This project sharpened how I design *for two audiences inside one system* and how much good UX in a regulated context is really about making constraints — routing, compliance, permissions — feel effortless to the people they protect. Designing against a real backend with a tech lead also kept me honest: the best flow is the one that ships.
