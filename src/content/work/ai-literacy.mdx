## Context

Middle schoolers already use AI daily — but almost none have been taught *how to think about it*. The goal wasn't a computer-science unit or a fear-mongering "AI is dangerous" lecture. It was three shifts in how students relate to the tool:

- Building **healthy skepticism** — why you shouldn't blindly trust an answer.
- Exposing **invisible bias** — that models inherit bias from their training data.
- Using **AI as a tool, not a crutch** — something people shape and direct.

## The problem

Existing framings all missed. A CS course is too technical; an "AI for productivity" course treats the tool as neutral and all-knowing; a fear-mongering course leaves students disempowered; a one-size-fits-all course ignores grade level. We needed a framing that made kids *critical and capable* at once.

**Our positioning:** AI is a **mirror of society** and a **malleable tool** — and skepticism is the desired outcome, not the byproduct.

## My role

I co-created the curriculum with Alexi Lee, drawing on AI-ethics education work from the MIT Media Lab. I worked on the activity design, the interactive prototype students actually touch, and synthesizing the teacher-validation feedback into changes.

## Process

We designed three activities, each targeting one core idea:

1. **Descriptive Prompting** — *precision drives output.* If you want AI to do something for you, you have to be specific.
2. **Identifying Bias** — who is affected by AI, and is it fair? Grounded in recommendation algorithms and facial recognition.
3. **Fact-Checking AI** — AI generates from patterns in its training data, not from understanding or lived experience.

<Figure placeholder="Activity screens from the interactive prototype (add exports)" wide />

**Then we tested it with real teachers** — the pivot point of the project. Rather than assume the framing landed, we ran preliminary validation with practicing educators across grade levels.

## What teachers told us

<blockquote>
"It makes the expectation and comparison more understandable, rather than abstract."
<br />— on grounding bias in a personal connection
</blockquote>

**Michael Kim** (math teacher, Fusion Academy) found it engaging and directly applicable, but flagged that the discussion format needed adjustment for his school's schedule — and that a personal connection to *someone students know* made bias "feel like a real problem, not a distant one."

**Marlene Goncalves** (4th-grade English, Brookline MA) surfaced that pacing was unpredictable across classrooms, the "Why It Matters" section could be too complex for younger grades, and a detective format would resonate with older students. She suggested giving students **pre-named bots to choose from** (e.g., a "Lying Bot" for hallucination) — a concrete idea we could design around.

## Key takeaways from validation

**What worked:** teaching critical thinking about AI through *engaging, hands-on* activities, and using personal connections and anecdotes to make abstract ideas concrete.

**What we'd work on:** tuning language to students' actual reading level, and designing for non-traditional classroom formats and unpredictable pacing.

## Outcome

A **live, interactive prototype** and a curriculum deck, validated by practicing teachers who found it engaging and applicable — and whose feedback directly reshaped the pacing, reading level, and activity framing.

**Honest gap:** validation was preliminary and qualitative. The obvious next step is a classroom pilot with pre/post measures of whether students actually became more skeptical and precise — the outcome the whole curriculum is built to produce.

## Reflection

This project is the clearest example of how I like to work: form a strong point of view, put a rough version in front of real users fast, and let their reactions — not my assumptions — decide what changes. It also reflects what I care about: technology that makes people more capable and more critical, not more dependent.
